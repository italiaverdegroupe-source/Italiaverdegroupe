A mature olive tree that has spent forty years in Tuscan soil has never met 48°C, has never been watered with anything brackish, and has never had to hold itself upright in a shamal. None of that makes it unsuited to the Gulf — olives have grown around the Mediterranean's hot, dry edges for millennia. It makes the first year the one that decides everything.

Here is what that year actually turns on, in the order it matters.

## The planting window is October to March, and it is not a preference

A tree planted in June spends its first weeks putting out roots in soil that is 40°C at spade depth, while its canopy loses water faster than a disturbed root system can replace it. It may survive. It will not establish, and the difference shows up two summers later as a tree that never really got going.

Planted between October and March, the same tree roots into cooling soil, comes into the heat already anchored, and meets its first summer with a root system that can supply the canopy. The window is the single biggest factor in the price of the tree being money well spent.

If a project timeline puts planting in May, the honest answer is to hold the tree in the nursery until autumn rather than put it in the ground and hope. We would rather say that before the order than explain it afterwards.

## Acclimatisation is not a delay in the delivery

A specimen arriving from Italy has come out of a European autumn, spent two to three weeks in a container, and landed in a climate with roughly double the evaporative demand it is used to. Putting it straight onto a site is asking it to do two hard things at once.

So it goes to a nursery first: partial shade, controlled irrigation, and a period to adjust before it moves again. Every specimen in our inventory carries the date it is cleared for planting, and nothing is quoted as available before that date. When a delivery date looks longer than a supplier's, that period is usually the difference — and it is the part that does not show up on an invoice.

## Water quality will decide more than watering schedule

Most irrigation in the Emirates is treated sewage effluent or desalinated blend, and both carry salts. Olives tolerate salinity better than almost anything else of their size, which is exactly why people stop paying attention to it.

Three things worth knowing:

- **Leaching matters more than frequency.** Short daily cycles keep the top of the root ball wet and concentrate salt below it. Longer, less frequent irrigation that moves water past the root zone takes the salt with it.
- **Test the water before the planting design, not after the symptoms.** An EC reading costs very little and changes what you should plant and how you should irrigate.
- **Drainage is part of salinity.** In compacted fill — which is most new UAE sites — water that cannot leave the pit leaves salt behind when it evaporates.

## The pit is usually the problem

Much of the ground on a new development is engineered fill: compacted, alkaline, and effectively a container with no drainage hole. A root ball dropped into a pit dug in that material sits in a bathtub.

Dig wider than deep — two to three times the root-ball width, no deeper than its height so the tree cannot settle below grade — break the sides so roots have somewhere to go, and check that the pit actually drains before the tree arrives. Fill it with water the day before. If it is still standing the next morning, the planting detail needs to change, not the irrigation schedule.

## Documentation is not paperwork

Live plants entering the UAE need a MOCCAE import permit and a phytosanitary certificate from the country of origin, and the permit is valid for a limited period. A permit that expires while a container is at sea means the consignment cannot clear, and a container of live trees sitting at a port is the most expensive thing that can happen to an order.

It is worth asking any supplier how they track permit validity against sailing dates. The answer tells you a good deal about how the rest will go.

## What this adds up to

An olive tree of any size is a twenty-year decision made in an afternoon. The tree itself is the part everyone looks at; the season, the water, the pit and the permit are what determine whether it is still there in twenty years.
